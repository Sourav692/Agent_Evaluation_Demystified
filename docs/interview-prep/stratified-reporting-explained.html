Stratified Reporting, Explained Simply

Why one overall score is the most dangerous number on your dashboard — and what to say about it in an interview.

Start with the table that explains everything

A support bot is tested on 40 questions. You have version 1 running, and version 2 ready to ship. Here is the full result.

GroupCasesv1 passesv2 passesChange
English2220  (91%)22  (100%)better
Spanish108  (80%)10  (100%)better
Hindi87  (88%)4  (50%)collapsed
Overall4035  (87.5%)36  (90%)better

The overall score went up. 87.5% to 90%. Every dashboard in the world shows that as green, and version 2 ships.

Hindi went from 88% to 50%. Nearly half of those customers now get a wrong answer. Nothing in the overall number tells you this happened — the improvement in English and Spanish more than paid for the collapse.

Stratified reporting is simply this: calculate the score separately for each group, and look at the groups — not the total.

The words, in plain English

A "stratum" is just a group of test cases that share something. All the Hindi questions. All the billing questions. All the questions where the right answer is "I don't know". It is a slice of your test set, nothing more. The plural is "strata", and that is the only bit of vocabulary here.

"Stratified reporting" means scoring each slice on its own. Instead of one number for everything, you get one number per slice, and you read all of them.

Why the average lies so easily

It is arithmetic, not bad luck. A group is only as loud in the average as it is big.

  • Hindi is 8 of 40 cases — 20% of the test set.
  • It fell by 37.5 points — from 7 passes out of 8, to 4.
  • The average only felt 20% of that fall, which is 7.5 points.
  • English and Spanish together gained 4 cases — worth 10 points of the overall — so the total still rose.

The smaller the group, the more completely the average buries it. A group that is 5% of your traffic can fail entirely and move the overall score by five points.

The analogy

A school announces that its average exam score went up this year. It did. What the average does not mention is that one class failed every subject, and the other classes improved enough to cover it. The number is true, and it is useless for the only decision that mattered.

How to choose your groups

Pick the ways your users actually differ. For a support bot, four usually matter:

Split byExample groupsCatches
LanguageEnglish · Spanish · HindiA language with thin documentation behind it
What the user wantsbilling · account · general question · escalationOne workflow breaking while the rest are fine
Question typesimple lookup · multi-step · answer not in the docsA bot that guesses instead of saying "I don't know"
Turnssingle message · a back-and-forthQuality falling apart after turn three

Report each split on its own. Do not combine them into "Hindi billing multi-step questions" — you will end up with twenty groups of one case each, and one case tells you nothing.

The rule to remember

Gate on the worst group, not the average. A release passes when every group clears its floor — not when the mean clears a line.

This is the whole point of doing it. Reporting by group and then still shipping on the average changes nothing.

The trap: groups that are too small

With 8 Hindi cases, one case flipping moves that group by 12.5 points. So a "15-point drop in Hindi" might be one case having a bad day, not a real regression.

The fix is not to ignore small groups. It is to report them and not block on them until they are big enough to mean something — and to write down that you are doing it, so nobody mistakes silence for safety.

Say it like this

"We report every group, but we only block on groups above a minimum size. Below that, one case flipping swings the number more than a real regression would, so blocking there would just be reacting to noise."

In the interview

The one-line answer

If they ask "how do you report evaluation results?"

"Never as one number. I tag every test case by language, intent and question type, score each group separately, and gate the release on the worst group rather than the mean. An average is the easiest place in the system to hide a failing segment."

The scenario they will give you

"The dashboard is green but customers are complaining."

"That is usually an averaging problem before it is a quality problem. I would split the results by user segment and workflow first — if one segment is failing and it is small enough, the overall score never moved. The second thing I would check is whether the test set contains those customers' questions at all, because a segment with no test cases cannot show up as failing."

The follow-up to expect

Almost always: "what if a group is too small to measure?" The answer is the one above — report, do not block, and say so out loud. It shows you have actually run one of these rather than read about it.

The sentence that lands

"Overall quality improved, the release was blocked, and the reason was a segment that is 4% of traffic and 100% of some customers' experience."

Three things to carry away

  • One number hides the thing you most need to see. The bigger the rest of your traffic, the better it hides.
  • Tag cases when you create them, not later. You cannot split a test set by a label it does not have.
  • Gate on the worst group. Reporting by group and shipping on the average is the same as not doing it.
