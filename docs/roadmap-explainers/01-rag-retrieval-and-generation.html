Level 1 Explainer: RAG (Retrieval and Generation)

A plain-language walkthrough of everything Level 1 covers, pulled together from the seven notebooks, the field guide, the three interactive labs, and the interview question bank — with nothing repeated twice. Level 0’s Explainer is the prerequisite: this file assumes you already have the five-surface map and the reference-based/referenceless split from there, and builds on top of them rather than re-deriving them.

Who this is for. You don’t need to know DeepEval, RAGAS, or LangGraph to follow this. Every technical term is defined in plain words the first time it shows up, and every number below is taken straight from this repo’s own notebooks and labs — nothing is invented.

Why this level exists

A RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) system answers a question in two steps: it retrieves a handful of text chunks from a document store, then it generates an answer using those chunks as its only source of truth. Several sections below (2, 4, and 5, specifically) return to the same toy question — “What is the time complexity of deleting a node from a binary search tree (BST)?” — because it makes the two-step failure obvious: the correct fact (“O(h), where h is the tree’s height”) either gets found or it doesn’t, and the answer either sticks to what was found or it doesn’t. Other sections use whichever worked example the source notebook built around (Apache Spark, Type 2 Diabetes symptoms, ocean levels) — each is reused as-is rather than replaced, since the point that example makes is specific to it.

Here is the failure that motivates this entire level: a user reports a wrong answer, and the reflexive diagnosis is “the model hallucinated.” That is often false. If the retriever never fetched the chunk that says “O(h)”, the generator did not hallucinate anything — it answered faithfully from a context that simply didn’t contain the fact. Faithfulness, answer relevancy, and every other generation metric are bounded by retrieval; measuring them while retrieval is broken tells you nothing about the generator. The fix is completely different in each case: a retrieval failure wants chunking, hybrid search, or reranking; a generation failure wants prompt or model changes. Guessing wrong costs a sprint.

So Level 1 is built as two toolkits, retrieval and generation, each with metrics that need labels and metrics that don’t — and then it shows the one failure so structural that neither toolkit, run without a reference answer, can ever catch it.

  • Retrieval metrics (Sections 2–3) score the box on the left: did the right chunks come back, and in a good order?
  • Generation metrics (Sections 4–5) score the box on the right: given whatever did come back, is the answer any good?
  • Every metric in this level takes its inputs from exactly one of these two boxes — which is why, later on, you can look at a table of scores and know immediately which half of the system to go fix.

Within each half, the metrics split again along the line Level 0 already drew: does the check need a human-written label or reference answer, or can it run on its own? Sections 2 and 4 are the label-needing (or reference-needing) checks; Sections 3 and (partly) 5 are the ones that manage without one.

2. Deterministic retrieval metrics: four numbers that are supposed to disagree

Notebook: 01_Retrieval_Metrics_Deterministic.ipynb · Lab: 01-deterministic-retrieval-metrics.html

Before any judge model, any API key, or any cost, there is a layer of retrieval evaluation that is pure arithmetic over labelled ground truth: a query, the chunk IDs your retriever returned in rank order, and the chunk IDs that are actually relevant (written down once, by a human, and reused across every eval run). It is the cheapest signal in the whole stack, and because retrieval is the first stage of the pipeline, it caps everything measured downstream.

The four metrics:

Metric Question it answers Penalizes Reach for it when
Precision@K Of the K chunks retrieved, how many are relevant? Noise in the retrieved set Context windows are expensive and clutter is diluting the prompt
Recall@K Of all the relevant chunks that exist, how many made the top K? Missing evidence You want to know the hard ceiling everything downstream is capped by
MRR (Mean Reciprocal Rank) 1 / the rank of the first relevant hit Burying the best chunk under noise There’s typically one best chunk and you care how early it lands
nDCG@K (normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain) Is the ranking good once relevance is a matter of degree, not just yes/no? Bad ordering among several relevant-but-unequal chunks Relevance is graded (0–3) and more than one relevant chunk can coexist

The formulas, in plain terms:

Precision@K = (relevant chunks in top K) / K
Recall@K    = (relevant chunks in top K) / (total relevant chunks that exist)
MRR         = 1 / (rank of the FIRST relevant chunk)
nDCG@K      = DCG@K / IDCG@K,   where DCG = Σ grade_i / log2(i + 2)

The one detail worth sitting with: IDCG is computed over the ideal ranking of every relevant chunk that exists — including ones the retriever never returned at all. That is why nDCG can stay capped below 1.0 no matter how you reorder what was retrieved.

The worked example

    RET --> R1["#1 chunk_3<br/>grade 0 · noise"]
    RET --> R2["#2 chunk_1<br/>grade 3 · relevant"]
    RET --> R3["#3 chunk_7<br/>grade 1 · not labelled relevant"]
    RET --> R4["#4 chunk_2<br/>grade 2 · relevant"]
    RET --> R5["#5 chunk_9<br/>grade 0 · noise"]

    style R2 fill:#D9E3DB,stroke:#2F6B4F

chunk_5 is the whole lesson: it’s labelled relevant, graded 3 (the highest grade), and the retriever never returned it — so it’s invisible to Precision and MRR, and it’s the single reason Recall and nDCG are capped no matter what happens to the other chunks.

At K=5: Precision@5 = 0.40 (2 of 5 retrieved chunks were relevant — chunk_1, chunk_2; 3 were noise), Recall@5 = 0.67 (2 of the 3 relevant chunks that exist were found; chunk_5 was missed entirely), MRR = 0.50 (the first relevant chunk, chunk_1, landed at rank 2, so 1/2), nDCG@5 = 0.51 (the graded penalty for chunk_1, grade 3, ranking below chunk_3, grade 0, is much larger than a small swap between two similarly-graded chunks would be).

What happens as K moves from 1 to 5:

K Precision@K Recall@K MRR nDCG@K
1 0.00 0.00 0.50 0.00
2 0.50 0.33 0.50 0.39
3 0.33 0.33 0.50 0.41
4 0.50 0.67 0.50 0.51
5 0.40 0.67 0.50 0.51

Three things worth noticing in that table: Precision is not monotonic — it drops at K=3 (a noise chunk enters) and recovers at K=4 (a relevant one does), so a single precision number without its K is meaningless. MRR is flat at 0.50 across every K — the first relevant chunk sits at rank 2 and no amount of retrieving more can move it; a flat, low MRR means a ranking problem (reranker), not a retrieve-more problem. Recall and nDCG both flatline after K=4 — that flatline is the signature of a chunk that isn’t reachable at all; raising K further buys tokens and latency and nothing else.

Reading the four together

What you see What it means Where to look
Low recall, any precision The answer isn’t in the prompt at all Chunking, embedding model, hybrid search, query rewriting
Good recall, low MRR It’s in the prompt, but buried Reranker / cross-encoder
Good recall, falling precision K is too large for this query Dynamic K, or retrieve wide and rerank narrow
Good recall and MRR, low nDCG Ranking ignores degrees of usefulness Graded labels + a ranking-aware retriever
Everything good, answer still wrong Retrieval is fine — the bug is downstream Sections 4–5’s generation metrics

When to use it, and when not to

Use deterministic retrieval metrics when you’re setting up evaluation for the first time (no keys, no cost, the highest-leverage day of work available), when you’re comparing retrievers or chunk sizes exactly and repeatably, or when you need a CI gate that will never flake — these numbers can’t disagree with themselves the way a judge model can. Don’t rely on them alone when you have no hand-labelled relevant_ids and can’t produce them at scale, when relevance is genuinely fuzzy or query-dependent, or when your golden set was built once and never refreshed — a stale eval set stays green while the real system’s quality drifts, because it has stopped measuring what users actually ask.

3. LLM-judged retrieval metrics: three metrics because there are three failures

Notebook: 02_Retrieval_Metrics_LLM_Judged.ipynb · Lab: 02-llm-judged-retrieval-metrics.html

Section 2’s metrics all need a hand-written relevant_ids list, per query, written by a person. That doesn’t scale to arbitrary production traffic. DeepEval’s three Contextual metrics ask the same “is retrieval working” question with an LLM judge standing in for the hand-labelled IDs — and, on purpose, they ask it three separate times, because averaging the three into one “retrieval score” is the fastest way to make a real bug invisible: precision can halve while the average barely moves, and nobody investigates.

A naming collision worth flagging early. Section 2 and this section both use the words “Precision” and “Recall” — but the underlying math is not the same. Contextual Relevancy turns out to be exactly Section 2’s Precision@K formula (relevant / total, unweighted, order-blind) under a different name. Contextual Precision is a genuinely different, rank-weighted metric — the same idea as Precision@K, but scored so that a relevant chunk near the top counts for more than the same chunk buried lower. That’s the entire reason reordering chunks (with nothing added or removed) can move Contextual Precision while leaving Contextual Relevancy untouched.

Metric Question Needs expected_output? Cares about order? Catches
Contextual Precision Are relevant chunks ranked above irrelevant ones? Yes Yes A good chunk buried under noise
Contextual Recall Does the context, as a whole, cover everything the expected answer needs? Yes No Missing information — retrieval found something, not enough
Contextual Relevancy Of everything retrieved, how much is actually on-topic? No No Noise diluting the context, independent of ranking or completeness

Precision cares about order. Recall cares about coverage. Relevancy cares about signal-to-noise. A retriever can fail any one of the three while passing the other two — which is exactly why the tutorial ships all three rather than one blended number.

One easy-to-miss detail about Contextual Recall: it checks the retrieved context against expected_output — the reference answer — not against what the generator actually said (actual_output). It’s asking “was enough fetched to support the full expected answer?”, regardless of whether the generator used all of it. That trips people up constantly.

The judge produces verdicts; arithmetic does the scoring

Contextual Precision = Σ (precision@i, for each position i holding a relevant chunk) / (number of relevant chunks)
Contextual Recall    = (claims in expected_output supported by the context) / (total claims)
Contextual Relevancy = (relevant chunks) / (total chunks retrieved)

The judge’s only job is to label each chunk relevant/irrelevant, or each claim supported/unsupported. Everything after that is deterministic math — which is why the same set of verdicts always produces the same score, but two separate judge runs can still disagree, because the verdicts themselves are non-deterministic (Level 0, §3).

One query, four retrieval outcomes

Question: “What is Apache Spark used for?”

Scenario Contextual Precision Contextual Recall Contextual Relevancy What moved
Healthy retrieval 1.00 1.00 0.67 — (baseline)
Bad ranking (same chunks, reordered) 0.58 1.00 0.67 Precision alone
Thin coverage (one perfect chunk only) 1.00 0.50 1.00 Recall alone
Noise dilution (1 relevant of 4 chunks) 0.33 1.00 0.25 Relevancy, dragging Precision with it

Three things easy to miss in that table:

  • Healthy retrieval scores only 0.67 on Relevancy. That is not a bug in the retriever — a perfect 1.00 on Relevancy usually means K was set too small, not that the retriever is flawless.
  • Thin coverage scores 1.00 on two of the three metrics. If Relevancy is the only metric you’re running (the only one that needs no reference), a retriever with a real coverage gap looks perfect.
  • Relevancy and Precision fail together under noise dilution, but they decouple under bad ranking — noise ranked above signal is simultaneously a ranking failure and a signal-to-noise failure, while a reordering with the same chunks present doesn’t touch Relevancy at all.

Deciding which of the three to run

    Q2 -->|yes| Q3{"Are the relevant chunks<br/>ranked above the noise?"}

Coverage is checked before ranking on purpose: there’s no point tuning a reranker over a context that never contained the answer in the first place.

The threshold trap

The lab’s “thin coverage” scenario lands Contextual Recall at exactly 0.50 — DeepEval’s default threshold. That’s dangerous for a concrete reason: the judge is non-deterministic, so one flipped claim-level verdict on a re-run flips the whole test case from pass to fail. A score sitting exactly on its threshold should be read as a coin toss, not a verdict — either move the threshold away from where your scores actually cluster, or re-run near-threshold cases and take a majority.

When to use it, and when not to

Reach for judged contextual metrics when you have production traffic and no labelled chunk IDs (Relevancy runs on anything), or when your golden set doesn’t cover the query shapes real users send and hand-labelling every new one isn’t realistic. Stay with Section 2’s deterministic metrics when you’re gating CI on a hard pass/fail line (judge non-determinism makes for a flaky gate), when comparing two retrievers head to head (deterministic metrics remove judge variance from the comparison entirely), or when cost matters at volume — three contextual metrics over 500 test cases is 1,500+ judge calls, before generation has even been measured.

4. Generation without a reference: three ways “the answer is wrong” can happen

Notebook: 03_Generator_Metrics_Referenceless.ipynb · Lab: 03-generator-metrics.html

Sections 2–3 asked whether the right chunks reached the prompt. This section asks the next question: given whatever was retrieved, is the generated answer any good — without a human-written reference answer for every query. These are the checks you can realistically run against live production traffic, because none of them need a labelled ground truth for the specific query being scored.

Metric Question Compares against
Answer Relevancy Does the answer actually address the question that was asked? input only
Faithfulness Is the answer grounded in what was retrieved, with no invented claims? retrieval_context
Hallucination Does the answer align with a separately-supplied, trusted context, regardless of what retrieval fetched? a curated context, not retrieval_context

Why “referenceless” doesn’t mean “compares against nothing.” A reference means one specific thing: a correct answer, written by a person, before the system ever ran (expected_output). retrieval_context is not that — it’s something the pipeline produced while answering, exactly like the generated answer itself. The mechanical test: could this metric score a brand-new production query with zero human preparation? Faithfulness and Answer Relevancy — yes, which is why they can run on live traffic. Answer Correctness (Section 5) — no, because a person has to have written down the right answer first.

One honest caveat. Hallucination’s trusted context is human-curated, so it isn’t free the way the other two are. It’s referenceless in the sense that matters — it needs no per-query answer key, just a maintained corpus of trusted source material — but that corpus has to be kept current, so it doesn’t scale to arbitrary traffic quite as freely as Faithfulness does. Source material also isn’t an answer key: it says what’s true, not what this specific answer should have said.

The judge doesn’t score the answer as one blob — it decomposes first, and the three metrics use different decompositions of the same text:

Faithfulness     = (claims supported by retrieval_context) / (total claims)
Answer Relevancy = (statements that address the input) / (total statements)
Hallucination     = (claims contradicting the trusted context) / (total claims)

An answer can have three grounded claims and only one on-topic statement — Faithfulness and Answer Relevancy are not just two takes on the same slicing of the text.

A gotcha worth memorizing: Hallucination’s score runs backwards. A higher score means more hallucination, the opposite convention from Faithfulness and Answer Relevancy, where higher is better. Always check a new metric’s score direction rather than assuming.

Telling the three failures apart

An answer can be wrong in three genuinely different ways, and only one of them is visible to any referenceless metric:

  • Faithful but irrelevant — perfectly grounded in the retrieved context, but answers a different question than the one asked.
  • Relevant but unfaithful — directly addresses the question, but invents facts that aren’t in the context.
  • Faithful to bad context — accurately reflects retrieved chunks that were themselves wrong. Faithfulness cannot catch this. Neither can Answer Relevancy. Only a reference-based check (Section 5) can.
flowchart TD
    A["actual_output"] --> Q1{"Does it address<br/>the input?"}
    Q1 -->|no| AR["Answer Relevancy fails<br/>→ prompt / instruction problem"]
    Q1 -->|yes| Q2{"Is every claim in<br/>retrieval_context?"}
    Q2 -->|no| FA["Faithfulness fails<br/>→ grounding / decoding problem"]
    Q2 -->|yes| Q3{"Was retrieval_context<br/>itself correct?"}
    Q3 -->|"referenceless metrics<br/>cannot ask this"| BLIND["BLIND SPOT<br/>faithful and wrong"]
    Q3 -->|"needs expected_output<br/>or a trusted context"| AC["Answer Correctness (§5)<br/>Hallucination"]

    style BLIND fill:#EDDCD8,stroke:#8C2F22
    style AC fill:#DADCD6,stroke:#3C4E51

This blind spot is structural, not a tuning problem. Faithfulness is defined relative to the retrieved context, so it has no way to evaluate whether that context was true in the first place.

Faithfulness vs. Hallucination — the distinction people get wrong:

Faithfulness Hallucination
Compares against retrieval_context — whatever this query’s retriever fetched a separate curated context, independent of retrieval
Score direction Higher is better Higher is worse
Changes per query? Yes — the baseline moves with retrieval No — the baseline is fixed
Catches A generator inventing beyond what it was given An answer contradicting known truth, however it got there
Blind to Retrieved context that was itself wrong Anything outside the curated context
Runs on live traffic Yes, nothing extra needed Only where you maintain a trusted corpus

Use Faithfulness to police the generator. Use Hallucination when you have an independent, trusted source and want to check the answer against it regardless of what retrieval returned.

When to use it, and when not to

Reach for referenceless metrics when monitoring production traffic with nobody writing reference answers, when catching decoding-level failures — invention, over-claiming, drifting off the question — or whenever you need volume, since they scale to any input at zero labelling cost. Watch specifically for Semantic Similarity as a stand-in for correctness (Section 5) and for vague GEval criteria (Section 6) — the specificity you put into a rubric is the specificity you get back.

A cheaper alternative for Answer Relevancy specifically. DeepEval also ships RAGASAnswerRelevancyMetric, a binding into RAGAS that scores relevancy with embedding cosine similarity instead of an LLM judge call — cheaper and faster, at the cost of missing subtler forms of “technically similar wording, wrong answer” that a judge would catch. It’s the same tradeoff Section 5 draws out fully for Semantic Similarity vs. Answer Correctness, just applied one metric earlier in the pipeline.

What each of the five metrics in this level structurally cannot catch, gathered in one place (the individual “blind to” details are scattered across Sections 3–5 above):

Metric Cannot catch
Contextual Precision / Recall / Relevancy (§3) See §3’s own triage table — each of the three is blind to the other two’s failure mode
Answer Relevancy A confidently wrong answer to the right question
Faithfulness Context that was itself wrong
Hallucination Anything outside the one curated context you supplied
Answer Correctness (§5) Any query with no written reference to check against
Semantic Similarity (§5) Wrong numbers and facts — it measures topic, not truth

5. Generation against a reference: Answer Correctness and Semantic Similarity

Notebook: 04_Generator_Metrics_Reference_Based.ipynb · Lab: 03-generator-metrics.html

Section 4’s three metrics all judge the answer on its own terms — grounded, on-topic, consistent with a trusted source. None of them ask the most direct question: is the answer actually correct, against a known-good reference? That is what this section covers, and it’s the only place in Level 1 that can catch “faithful to bad context.”

  • Answer Correctness has no dedicated metric class. That’s not an oversight — “is it factually right” is specific to what “right” means for your task, so the recommended pattern is a custom GEval rubric rather than one generic class (Section 6 covers how to write one).
  • Answer Semantic Similarity isn’t an LLM-judged metric at all — it’s plain embedding cosine similarity between the answer and the reference. Cheap, fast, no judge call — and weaker at catching a subtly wrong fact, because two answers can sit close in embedding space while disagreeing on the one number that actually matters.
  • Both need expected_output — a reference answer a person had to write. That’s the precise cost side of the reference-based/referenceless tradeoff from Level 0: exact, but only as scalable as your ability to write and keep reference answers current.

The same four answers, now with reference-based scores added

Question: “What is the time complexity of deleting a node from a BST?” Reference: “O(h), where h is the height of the tree.”

Answer Answer Relevancy Faithfulness Hallucination ↓ Answer Correctness Semantic Similarity
Grounded & relevant 1.00 1.00 0.00 0.95 0.94
Faithful but irrelevant 0.33 1.00 0.00 0.35 0.78
Relevant but unfaithful 1.00 0.50 0.50 0.40 0.91
Faithful to bad context 1.00 1.00 1.00 0.10 0.83

Read the last row carefully. The retriever returned a chunk claiming BST deletion is O(1). The generator repeated it accurately. Every claim is supported by the context it was handed, and the answer directly addresses the question — so both referenceless metrics report a perfect score on an answer that is simply false. Only Answer Correctness, checked against the reference, catches it: 0.10.

The Semantic Similarity column carries its own warning: 0.83 for an answer that says O(1) where the truth is O(h). Embeddings measure topic, and “constant time” and “logarithmic time” sit topically close together — similarity will happily score a wrong answer as “pretty good.” Pair Semantic Similarity with a judged correctness check; never let it substitute for one.

Two ways to write the correctness rubric

GEval (the tool behind Answer Correctness) takes exactly one of two configurations — never both at once, it’s one or the other:

  • criteria — a short, plain-language sentence (“Determine whether actual_output is factually correct relative to expected_output, focusing on whether key facts match — wording may differ”), which the judge expands into its own evaluation steps internally.
  • evaluation_steps — an explicit, ordered procedure you write yourself (e.g. “1. list the statements in actual_output, 2. check each is relevant to input, 3. check each agrees with expected_output, 4. check each is grounded in retrieval_context, 5. penalize anything invented”), which lets you weave several test-case fields — input, expected answer, and retrieved context — into one rubric at once.

evaluation_steps is the more controllable of the two: instead of trusting the judge to expand a one-line criteria into a good procedure, you write the procedure. Section 6 explains why that control matters so much.

When to use it, and when not to

Gate CI where you control the test set and want a hard pass/fail bar. Reach for reference-based checks specifically when the failure you fear is wrongness, not ungroundedness — which covers every index-corruption and stale-document scenario Section 4 structurally cannot see. Use them when comparing prompt or model versions, where a fixed reference makes the comparison meaningful. And always watch the two traps: Semantic Similarity standing in for correctness, and a criteria sentence too vague to say what should fail (Section 6).

6. Writing a rubric that doesn’t produce a vague judge

Field guide §1.5

Every custom GEval — Answer Correctness above, or any other rubric you write — comes down to one fact: you are either writing the test, or letting the judge invent it for you.

You give the judge What happens Result
One sentence — “check the answer is accurate” The judge has to invent its own evaluation steps, and invents slightly different ones on every run The same answer gets different scores on different runs
Explicit steps — “1. every figure appears in the context, 2. it answers the question asked, 3. no advice beyond the policy” The judge follows the procedure you wrote The same answer gets the same score every time
  • Write explicit steps whenever a one-liner won’t pin down what should fail. Steps also let you check several things in one pass — relevance, agreement with a reference, and grounding — instead of running three separate metrics.
  • A vague rubric never errors. Nothing goes red. You just get numbers that wobble slightly from run to run, with no signal pointing at the rubric as the cause. That’s what makes vague rubrics dangerous: they fail silently, as noise, not as a visible break.
  • The test to apply to your own rubric: if you can’t say precisely what would make an answer fail it, neither can the judge.

The same idea shows up outside AI evaluation constantly: handing a human grader “mark this fairly” instead of a marking scheme produces five different grades from five different graders, every one of them individually defensible.

7. Evaluation as pipeline gates, not just after-the-fact scoring

Notebook: 05_RAG_Eval_Inside_the_Pipeline.ipynb

Every metric in Sections 2–5 scores a RAG system’s output after the fact — build a test case, hand it to a metric, read a score. That’s how you evaluate offline, against a batch of test cases. But context relevance and faithfulness are exactly the kind of check that can also run as part of the pipeline itself, gating generation on bad retrieval, or flagging a hallucinated answer, before either one ever reaches a user.

flowchart LR
    RET["retrieve"] --> JCR["judge_context_relevance<br/>(§3's Contextual Relevancy,<br/>computed by hand)"]
    JCR --> GEN["generate<br/>(fed only the relevant chunks)"]
    GEN --> CF["check_faithfulness<br/>(§4's Faithfulness,<br/>computed by hand)"]

    style JCR fill:#D9E3DB,stroke:#2F6B4F
    style CF fill:#EDE3CE,stroke:#9A6A12

Two reasons this pattern is worth knowing, beyond it being a different way to compute the same numbers:

  1. You can act on the result mid-pipeline. judge_context_relevance runs before generation — cheap enough, and early enough, that a real system could branch on it: skip generation and ask a clarifying question instead of generating a plausible-sounding answer from noise, if nothing relevant came back.
  2. It demystifies the metric classes. Building “context relevance” and “faithfulness” out of raw judge calls once, by hand, makes it far clearer what a ready-made metric class like ContextualRelevancyMetric is actually doing underneath.

The worked example retrieves three chunks for the BST question, one of them deliberately off-topic (a fact about Python lists). The mock judge correctly flags the off-topic chunk irrelevant, filters it out before generation runs, and the resulting answer scores Faithfulness 1.00 — no hallucination detected, because it was only ever given the chunks that actually supported an answer.

Prototype against mocked judges first. Same graph structure, byte-identical output, zero cost — build and debug the pipeline’s shape this way. Only once that shape is right do you swap in real judge calls, and that swap is exactly where non-determinism becomes visible: the same graph, run twice against a live model, can produce slightly different judgments and slightly different extracted claims each time. That’s why production evals typically run a judge at temperature=0 (as deterministic as a model can be made) and sometimes track judge-agreement itself as a metric, rather than trusting any single pass.

8. RAGAS as a second opinion, and the pytest/CI batch pattern

Notebook: 06_RAGAS_in_Practice.ipynb

RAGAS is a second framework — narrower in scope than DeepEval, RAG-specific, and a common default in the wider RAG ecosystem — that measures the same ideas from Sections 3–4 in its own vocabulary:

RAGAS class The DeepEval idea it mirrors Note
LLMContextPrecisionWithoutReference Contextual Precision (§3) The WithoutReference suffix spells the reference-based/referenceless split out in the class name itself, rather than leaving you to infer it from which fields you populate
LLMContextRecall Contextual Recall (§3)
Faithfulness Faithfulness (§4)

Two mechanical differences worth knowing if you switch between the two libraries: RAGAS’s unit of evaluation is a SingleTurnSample (fields user_input, response, retrieved_contexts, reference) rather than DeepEval’s LLMTestCase — same idea, different field names — and RAGAS is async-first (await sample.single_turn_ascore(...), or asyncio.run(...)), where DeepEval defaults to a synchronous .measure(). RAGAS also wants an explicit LangchainLLMWrapper around any LangChain-compatible chat model to act as its judge, rather than resolving a judge from a model="..." string the way DeepEval does — a small extra step that buys the ability to use any LangChain-integrated provider as the judge, not only OpenAI models by name.

On a sample built so retrieval pulls in one irrelevant chunk alongside a correct one, Context Precision comes back lower than Context Recall — the same signature Section 3 already taught you to read: precision is penalized directly by noise sitting in the ranking, while recall (checked against the reference, which the irrelevant chunk doesn’t contradict) is far less affected by it.

The batch/CI pattern

A single hand-built test case teaches you what a metric does. It says nothing about your system. Turning “one scored example” into “a real evaluation” is always the same shape:

dataset of inputs → call the live system once per row → score the real response → write the score back beside its input

The production version of this in the repo’s source material is a pytest-parametrized test that reads questions from a spreadsheet, calls a live RAG backend for each one, scores the response with RAGAS, writes the score back into the same spreadsheet next to the question that produced it, and asserts the score as a CI gate. pytest.mark.parametrize is what turns “one eval sample” into “one test per row” — pytest itself becomes the batch runner and the pass/fail reporter, with no separate eval-harness loop to write. This is a general pattern, not specific to RAGAS or to this one notebook — Level 1’s own capstone (Section 9) runs a version of it, and Level 2 reuses the exact same shape for tool-use and task-completion metrics.

Why every row has to hit the live system matters: scores land right next to the input that produced them (a bad number is one click from its cause — a dashboard, not just a diagnosis), and the whole thing re-runs on demand, which is the actual prerequisite for comparing a change against what’s currently live.

9. Building a RAG system and synthesizing a golden set

Notebook: 07_RAG_Capstone_Build_and_Evaluate.ipynb · Field guide §1.8

Every notebook up to this point either used hand-constructed test cases (Sections 2–6) or fixture data in a toy graph (Section 7). The capstone does the real thing:

flowchart TD
    D["10 real documents<br/>(a small CSV)"] --> IDX["Indexed into Chroma<br/>(a vector store)"]
    IDX --> RC["A real retriever + generator chain<br/>(the actual RAG system)"]
    D --> SYN["Synthesizer generates<br/>question + reference-answer pairs<br/>FROM the documents"]
    SYN --> GOLD["Golden dataset"]
    GOLD -->|"each golden question run through"| RC
    RC --> TC["Test cases built from LIVE retrieval,<br/>not from the golden's source doc"]
    TC --> SUITE["Full metric suite<br/>(§2–§5, all at once)"]
    SUITE --> TABLE["One row per question,<br/>one column per metric"]

    style SYN fill:#EDE3CE,stroke:#9A6A12
    style TC fill:#D9E3DB,stroke:#2F6B4F

Hand-writing reference answers doesn’t scale past a handful of examples. A synthesizer generates question/expected-answer pairs directly from your own documents instead, evolved along dimensions you choose — for instance, requiring multi-step reasoning, or requiring information pulled from more than one chunk at once — so you get a labelled eval set without writing a single reference answer by hand. Spot-check the output anyway: a generated question is only as good as the generation process that produced it.

The one detail that makes this an honest end-to-end test, not a generator-only test: each golden question’s test case is built by actually running the live retriever on that question — not by reusing the document the golden question was originally generated from. If the retriever performs badly on a given question, its retrieval_context reflects that badly, and the evaluation below is testing the real, currently-deployed pipeline, not just checking whether the generator can write a good answer given hand-picked, perfect context.

Running the full metric suite together — retrieval and generation, reference-based and referenceless, all at once — over every golden question is how a RAG quality report actually gets read in practice: an aggregate table (mean, min, max per metric), not one hand-picked example eyeballed at a time. Which is exactly what Section 10 explains how to read.

10. Reading the results

Field guide §1.9

A table of per-metric scores across a golden set is diagnostic, not just descriptive — if you read it right.

  • Diagnose by pattern, not by the headline number. Low Contextual Precision with high everything else points at a ranking problem (Section 3). Low Faithfulness alongside high Contextual Relevancy points at the generator inventing things despite good retrieval (Section 4). The shape of a scorecard carries more information than any single number in it.
  • Aggregates hide the thing you need to see. An 85% average with one query category sitting at zero, and an 85% average spread evenly across every category, are two entirely different systems reporting the identical headline score.
  • Slice before you conclude. Break scores down by query category, tenant, intent, or document type before deciding the system is “fine” or “broken” — an aggregate can hide a segment that’s failing completely.

Question-bank corner — retrieval and generation numbers, live on a dashboard

(From QUESTION_BANK.md, Part 10, §10.1–§10.3 — the three sections that map onto Level 1’s three labs. §10.4 onward belongs to Level 2’s tool-use and trajectory material and isn’t repeated here.)

These aren’t the conceptual questions Sections 1–10 already answered — they’re the follow-up questions asked once an interviewer has your metrics dashboard on screen, phrased the way a production, real-time interview round (OpenAI-, Anthropic-, Google-caliber) tends to phrase them. A representative sample from each of the three sections:

  • §10.1 (deterministic retrieval): “Leadership wants one number for retrieval quality on an exec dashboard — what do you put there, and what do you say about the request?” A strong answer picks Recall@K if forced (it’s the ceiling everything downstream is capped by), states plainly what that one number hides (a good recall figure is compatible with the right answer sitting at rank 8 under seven pieces of noise), and proposes Recall@K on the dashboard with Precision@K and MRR one click away, rather than either refusing the request or pretending one number is enough.
  • §10.2 (LLM-judged retrieval): “Contextual Precision drops from 0.91 to 0.62 after a release; Recall and Relevancy are unchanged. What shipped?” The signature reads directly off Section 3’s table: the same chunks are still being retrieved (recall didn’t move) but their order changed — so the likely cause is a reranker change, a scoring/fusion weight change, or how ties get broken, not an embedding or chunking change.
  • §10.3 (generator metrics): “Faithfulness and Answer Relevancy both sit above 0.95 for a month, then a customer proves the assistant has been giving wrong compliance guidance the whole time. How is that possible?” This is Section 4/5’s blind spot, verbatim: both metrics are referenceless, so a knowledge-base document that is itself wrong produces a perfectly faithful, perfectly relevant, wrong answer — no threshold on either metric could have caught it, because it’s a structural blind spot, not a calibration failure. The fix is reference-based coverage (Section 5) for the high-stakes slice, plus an upstream fix: index freshness monitoring, since the root cause lives in the knowledge base, not the model.

The full sections (§10.1 has 5 questions, §10.2 has 5, §10.3 has 4) walk through the reasoning for each in more depth than fits here — this corner is a map of what kind of question to expect, not a substitute for reading them.
