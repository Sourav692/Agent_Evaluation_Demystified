Level 5 Explainer: Statistics and stratified reporting

A plain-language walkthrough of everything Level 5 covers, pulled together from the notebook, the field guide, the interactive lab, and the interview question bank — with nothing repeated twice.

Who this is for. You don’t need any statistics background to follow this. Every technical term — sample size, paired comparison, bootstrap, confidence interval — is defined in plain words the first time it shows up.

Why this level exists

A support bot is tested on 40 questions, in English, Spanish and Hindi. Version 1 is live; version 2 is ready to ship. Here is the full result:

Group Cases v1 passes v2 passes Change
English 22 20 (91%) 22 (100%) better
Spanish 10 8 (80%) 10 (100%) better
Hindi 8 7 (88%) 4 (50%) collapsed
Overall 40 35 (87.5%) 36 (90%) better

The overall score went up — 87.5% to 90%. Every dashboard in the world shows that as green, and version 2 ships. But Hindi went from 88% to 50%: nearly half of those customers now get a wrong answer. Nothing in the overall number tells you this happened, because the improvement in English and Spanish more than paid for the collapse. Hindi is only 8 of 40 cases — 20% of the test set — so the average only felt 20% of its 37.5-point fall, which is 7.5 points, and that got buried under 10 points of gain from the other two groups.

This is arithmetic, not bad luck. A group is only as loud in the average as it is big. A group that is 5% of your traffic can fail entirely and move the overall score by five points.

Level 5 is the discipline that stops this from shipping: report every score by the group it belongs to, not just as one blended number; know, before you argue about a small move, whether your test set is even large enough to see it; and when you do compare two versions on the same cases, use the test built for paired data instead of throwing that pairing away.

1. Stratified reporting: score each slice, not the whole

A stratum (plural: strata) is just a group of test cases that share something — all the Hindi questions, all the billing questions, all the ones where the right answer is “I don’t know.” It’s a slice of your test set, nothing more. Stratified reporting means scoring each slice on its own, instead of collapsing everything into one number, and reading all of them.

How to choose your groups. Pick the ways your users actually differ. For a support bot, four usually matter:

Split by Example groups Catches
Language English · Spanish · Hindi A language with thin documentation behind it
What the user wants billing · account · general question · escalation One workflow breaking while the rest are fine
Question type simple lookup · multi-step · answer not in the docs A bot that guesses instead of saying “I don’t know”
Turns single message · a back-and-forth Quality falling apart after turn three

The analogy. A school announces that its average exam score went up this year. It did. What the announcement doesn’t mention is that one class failed every subject, and the other classes improved enough to cover it. The number is true, and it’s useless for the only decision that mattered.

Tag cases when you create them, not later. You can’t split a test set by a label it doesn’t have. This cuts both ways when a dashboard looks green but customers are still complaining: split the results by segment first, but also check whether the test set contains those customers’ questions at all — a segment with zero test cases can’t show up as failing, so a gap in coverage and a genuinely healthy segment look identical unless you check.

Report each split on its own — never cross-tabulate. It’s tempting to slice by language and intent and question type at once, but with 40 cases and three dimensions you get mostly empty cells, and an empty cell is where somebody makes a confident statement about a segment of size one. Don’t combine splits into “Hindi billing multi-step questions” — you’ll end up with twenty groups of one case each, and one case tells you nothing.

Every mean needs its n printed beside it. A mean without a sample size is a rumor. The notebook’s own report format makes this literal — it prints, for every stratum and every metric, both the average and how many cases that average was computed from, because different metrics can be missing for different subsets of the same stratum (a stratum with 12 cases overall might have a faithfulness score for only 3 of them).

“Never as one number. I tag every test case by language, intent and question type, score each group separately, and gate the release on the worst group rather than the mean. An average is the easiest place in the system to hide a failing segment.”

The rule to remember, and the point of doing any of this: gate on the worst group, not the average. A release passes when every group clears its floor — not when the mean clears a line. Reporting by group and then still shipping on the average changes nothing; it’s the same mistake with an extra step.

2. Sample size: could your suite even see the change?

Before arguing about whether a 4-point move is real, ask a cheaper question first: could a test set this size ever detect a move that small? The rule-of-thumb formula for a proportion, at 95% confidence and 80% power (“power” is the chance you’d actually notice a real effect if one were there):

n ≈ 15.7 · p(1 − p) / δ²

where p is roughly where you are now and δ (delta) is the size of the change you want to be able to see. (15.7 comes from 2 × (1.96 + 0.84)² — the standard two-sided, 95%-confidence, 80%-power constants — so this is a documented rule of thumb, not a substitute for a full power analysis, but it’s the right order of magnitude instantly.)

A worked example: at p = 0.80 and wanting to see a 10-point move (δ = 0.10), you need n ≈ 251 cases. A 40-case suite, by contrast, can see a collapse, but not a slip — it simply doesn’t have the resolution for small moves. The same formula runs in reverse, too: given the n you actually have, it tells you the smallest change you’re capable of detecting at all (δ ≈ √(15.7 · p(1−p) / n)). Publish that number next to every report. It turns “the score dropped 4 points” from a fire drill into a known, honest limitation of the suite — and it’s the honest answer to “do you ship?” when someone asks about a move that’s actually inside your own blind spot.

This same arithmetic is the reason a dashboard sampling live traffic can miss a real regression — see Section 6 below.

3. The small-stratum rule: when a group is too small to gate on

Sample-size math cuts hardest against your smallest groups. With 8 Hindi cases, one case flipping moves that group by 12.5 points — each case is worth 100% / 8 = 12.5 percentage points on its own. So a “15-point drop in Hindi” might be one case having a bad day, not a real regression.

The fix is not to ignore small groups — it’s to report them and not block on them, until they’re big enough to mean something, and to say out loud that you’re doing it. A stratum below that minimum size still gets its number printed on the dashboard; it just isn’t allowed to fail a release on its own. Silently dropping it would hide the very customers it’s meant to protect; silently gating on it would block releases at random.

“We report every group, but we only block on groups above a minimum size. Below that, one case flipping swings the number more than a real regression would, so blocking there would just be reacting to noise.”

This is exactly the argument behind the release gate’s min_cases_to_gate setting (covered in full in Level 6): the per-stratum detection-limit table — n, what one case is worth, and the smallest change that stratum can detect at all — is what tells you where to draw that line, not a round number picked in advance.

The three tools from this level, aimed at exactly this scenario, agree with each other. Take the smallest stratum in a test set, flip a single case, and check it three ways: the bootstrap confidence intervals before and after the flip overlap almost completely; McNemar’s test on the same before/after pairing finds nothing significant. All three readings say the same thing — a single flipped case is statistical noise, not a regression — which is exactly why the gate reports a small stratum’s number instead of blocking on it.

4. Paired comparison and McNemar’s test

You don’t compare version 1 and version 2 on two different, unrelated samples — you run the exact same golden cases against both. That pairing is information, and treating the two runs as independent throws it away.

McNemar’s test looks only at the cases that changed between the two runs:

                 candidate
                pass    fail
  baseline pass   b       c      <- c: regressions (used to pass, now fails)
           fail   d       a      <- d: fixes       (used to fail, now passes)

  statistic = (|c − d| − 1)² / (c + d)      ~ chi-square, 1 degree of freedom
  significant (p < 0.05) when the statistic exceeds 3.84

Cases that landed in the same place both times (a and b above — pass→pass and fail→fail) carry no information about a change, and McNemar deliberately ignores them. That’s exactly why a paired test needs far fewer cases than an unpaired one to see the same effect: an unpaired comparison of two independent proportions has to fight through the noise of all 40 cases, while McNemar only has to look at the handful that actually flipped.

The notebook demonstrates this on a constructed example: take the golden set’s pass/fail results as the baseline, then build a “candidate” by deliberately breaking 5 previously-passing cases and fixing 2 previously-failing ones, and run McNemar on the pairing. With c + d that small, the test may well come back not-significant — and that is the correct answer, not a failure of the test. A handful of flips in either direction, on a 40-case suite, is exactly what you’d expect to see from chance alone.

5. Confidence intervals: bootstrap and Wilson

A single score — “90% resolved” — is a point estimate. It doesn’t say how much that number would move if you’d happened to draw a different 40 cases. A confidence interval answers that: a range that the true rate probably falls inside, given the sample you actually have. This level covers two different ways to build one.

The bootstrap

The bootstrap answers “how much would this number move if I’d drawn a different sample of the same size?” by literally simulating that, thousands of times: resample your existing cases with replacement (the same case can be picked more than once, and some cases won’t be picked at all), recompute the statistic on each resample, and take the middle 95% of the resulting spread as the interval.

It’s deliberately implemented in about ten lines of plain random and statistics — no scipy, no pandas. The point of writing it by hand, rather than importing it, is that a confidence interval you can’t derive is a confidence interval you’ll misread. The bootstrap’s real advantage is that it works for any statistic — a mean, a median, a rate — because it never assumes a particular bell-curve shape for the underlying data; it just resamples what you actually observed.

Read the widths, not the points. A stratum whose interval spans 0.4 isn’t telling you it’s worse than another stratum — it’s telling you that you don’t know, because the sample is too small to say anything precise.

Wilson confidence intervals

For the specific, common case of a proportion (a pass rate, a resolution rate), there’s a closed-form formula — no resampling required — called the Wilson interval. It’s a standard statistical technique for putting a confidence interval around a proportion that stays well-behaved even at small sample sizes or when the observed rate is close to 0% or 100%, where a naive bell-curve approximation breaks down. This repo doesn’t reproduce the formula itself, but it does show what it resolves to in practice, at an observed rate of 0.95:

Traces scored Resolution (± points)
50 ±6.6
100 ±4.5
200 ±3.1
600 ±1.8
2,000 ±1.0

Read this the same way as the bootstrap widths above: it’s the size of the wobble your number carries purely from which cases happened to be sampled, before you’ve asked anything about whether the system actually changed.

Bootstrap vs. Wilson, in one line: Wilson is a fast, closed-form shortcut that only works for proportions; the bootstrap is slower but works for any statistic, and — because you write it yourself — never leaves you unsure what it’s actually claiming. Either one is answering the same question: how much could this number move on a different sample?

6. The same problem in production: the sampling-resolution problem

The Wilson-interval table above isn’t just an offline concern — it’s exactly the arithmetic behind a very common production mistake: grading a sample of live traffic instead of every request, because grading everything with an LLM judge costs real money.

The setup. Your agent handles 2,000 conversations a day. You grade 100 of them — a 5% sample — and assume the rest look about the same. But the number wobbles purely from which 100 you happened to grade:

Day 1:  you grade 100 conversations → 95 pass → you report 95%
Day 2:  you grade a different 100    → 92 pass → you report 92%

Nothing changed. Same agent, same code, same prompt.
Only WHICH 100 you happened to grade changed.

That’s a ±4.5-point wobble — the same number the Wilson table above gives for n=100. So whether you can see a real problem depends entirely on how big the problem is relative to that wobble:

An alert set at 2 points, at a 5% sample rate, is worse than no alert at all — two ordinary, unchanged days can differ by several points from luck alone, so the alert fires on days when nothing broke. A team that gets paged for nothing learns to close the page without looking, which is exactly the credibility cost this whole level is trying to avoid.

To detect a 2-point drop you need about 606 scored traces — at 2,000 traces/day, that’s a 30% sample rate, not the 5% that felt affordable. And if a delta can’t be resolved even at 100% sampling, the answer isn’t more sampling — it’s to move that comparison offline, where every input is scored and, crucially, the comparison is paired: the exact same fixed set of questions asked of the old build and the new one, which removes the day-to-day variation in what customers happened to ask as a confound (this is the same paired-vs- unpaired idea behind McNemar in Section 4 — a fixed, shared set of cases is what lets you attribute a difference to the change, rather than to which traffic showed up that day).

The analogy. Tasting a pot of soup. One spoonful tells you instantly if it’s far too salty; it can never tell you whether today’s pot is 2% saltier than yesterday’s — especially when it’s a different pot of soup every day.

The decision this leaves you with, as a repeatable procedure:

  1. Decide the smallest regression you must catch.
  2. Look up (or compute, via Section 5’s formulas) the samples needed for that resolution.
  3. Divide by daily traffic to get the sample rate.
  4. Deterministic scorers (checks code can run, not a judge) never need sampling at all — they’re free, so score every request.

Name the smallest regression you refuse to miss first, then buy the sample rate that can actually see it — and if no sample rate can, that comparison belongs offline, not on a dashboard.

7. Simpson’s paradox: when every stratum improves and the total falls

This isn’t a trick question — it’s a real, mechanical consequence of stratified reporting that every stratum can genuinely improve while the overall number falls, whenever a release also shifts the mix of traffic between strata. A constructed illustration:

Stratum Before After Change
English (n: 90 → 20) 90.00% 95.00% +5.00
Hindi (n: 10 → 80) 50.00% 55.00% +5.00
Overall 86.00% 63.00% −23.00

Every single stratum went up by 5 points. The overall number collapsed by 23 points — because the mix of who’s asking moved from 90% English / 10% Hindi to 20% English / 80% Hindi, and Hindi is the weaker segment regardless of which version is live. A gate on the aggregate alone would block a release that made every group better; a gate on the worst stratum would correctly pass it. This is the single best argument for gating per stratum rather than on the mean, and it’s the reason the release gate (Level 6) has a dedicated check for exactly this case, separate from its overall-average check.

8. Putting it together

Four small tools, four different questions, and none of them substitutes for another:

Tool Question it answers
Stratified grouping Which segment is bad?
n ≈ 15.7·p(1−p)/δ² Could this suite even see that change?
McNemar Did the same cases really get worse, or did a handful just flip by chance?
Bootstrap / Wilson interval How much would this number move on a different sample?

And the two decision rules that sit on top of all four: gate on the worst stratum, not the average (Sections 1 and 7), and report a small stratum’s number without blocking a release on it (Section 3). Do all of this before the release gate itself, covered next in Level 6 — this level is what makes that gate’s numbers trustworthy in the first place.

Put in one sentence, this level’s whole job is: refuse to act on a number you cannot resolve, and never report an average that buries a segment. If you’re preparing this material for an interview-style round, Simpson’s paradox (Section 7) is the strongest follow-up to have ready — it’s the one case where the aggregate and every individual stratum genuinely disagree, and the aggregate is the one that’s wrong.

Question-bank corner — the two rounds this level prepares you for

(From QUESTION_BANK.md — Q4 and Q21, the two questions in the bank that this level’s material speaks to directly; the rest of the bank belongs to other levels.)

Q4 — “The customer wants one number for a dashboard. What do you give them?” This is Section 1’s argument, aimed at an executive audience: two systems — one slightly mediocre everywhere, one excellent everywhere except that it leaks account data under an adversarial prompt — can produce the identical headline average. The strong answer restructures the dashboard into three layers instead of fighting the request for “one number”: a headline that’s a pass/fail verdict against the blocking gates (not an average — a verdict can’t be rescued by being good at something else), the slices underneath it (by category, by whether a tool was involved, by attack type, by turn), and a separate “not measured” column, because an unmeasured dimension and a failed one must never look the same — reporting a gap as a zero makes a healthy system look broken, and hiding it makes a blind spot look like coverage. The line worth having ready: “the headline tells you whether to look, the slices tell you where.”

Q21 — “A metric dropped two points this week. Walk me through what you do.” This is Sections 2 and 6 turned into an investigation checklist. The first question is never “what caused it” — it’s “can this sample even resolve two points?” (Section 6’s whole argument). If the drop clears the noise floor and is real, three things could have changed, and they need separating: us (a prompt, model, retrieval config or tool — check the deployment timeline first, because it’s cheapest to confirm or rule out), them (the traffic mix shifted — slice by category; a drop concentrated in one category almost always means this, while a drop spread evenly points at “us”), or the measurement itself (a judge model got upgraded underneath you, or the scored population changed — rare, and easy to miss, because in that case the system never moved at all). Then reproduce it offline, where the comparison is controlled and paired (Section 6 again), confirm it, fix it, and leave the case behind as a permanent regression test. The line worth having ready: “paging someone about noise is how you teach a team to ignore the alert — that costs more than the drop.”
